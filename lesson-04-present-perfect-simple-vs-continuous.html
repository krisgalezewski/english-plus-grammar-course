Present Perfect Simple vs. Present Perfect Continuous

Counting the result — or picturing the activity itself.

1 Warm-up — building on Lesson 3

Lesson 3 showed that present perfect connects a past action to right now. Today we split present perfect into two flavours — one for the finished result, one for the ongoing activity itself.

1. "I have written three reports today" mainly tells us…

2. "I have been writing reports all day" mainly tells us…

3. So which form usually goes with a specific count or number?

💡 Both forms connect the past to now — the difference is whether you're pointing at the finished result or at the activity itself.

3 The concept

Result, or activity?

Present Perfect Simple points at a finished result or a countable amount. Present Perfect Continuous points at the activity itself — how it's been going, regardless of whether it's finished. Browse each below, or test yourself with Quick check.

✅ Present Perfect Simple: subject + have/has + past participle — e.g. "She has written the report."
✅ Present Perfect Continuous: subject + have/has + been + verb-ing — e.g. "She has been writing the report since 9am."

Present Perfect Simple

Present Perfect Continuous

🎯 The tricky part: both forms can use "for" or "since," so duration alone doesn't decide the tense — it depends on whether you're counting a result or describing the process.

✅ "I have read three chapters this afternoon." — a countable result. Simple.
✅ "I have been reading all afternoon." — the activity itself, no count. Continuous.

Words like "already," "just," and an exact number almost always signal simple; words like "all day," "lately," and a visible present effect almost always signal continuous. Watch for this exact contrast in the practice section below.

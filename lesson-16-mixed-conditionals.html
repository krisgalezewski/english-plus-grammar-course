Mixed Conditionals

When the if-clause and the result clause point to two different times.

1 Warm-up — two conditionals, one sentence

So far, every conditional sentence has kept the same time frame in both clauses. Mixed conditionals break that rule on purpose — a past condition can have a present result, or a present condition can have a past result.

1. Mixed conditionals combine…

2. "If I had learned to swim as a child, I wouldn't be afraid of water now" mixes…

3. "If she weren't so shy, she would have spoken up yesterday" mixes…

💡 Ask two questions: when is the CONDITION true (past, or an ongoing trait/state)? And when does the RESULT apply (past, or now)? The answers decide which mix you need.

2 & 3 — an ongoing condition, a past result

It helps to think of this if-clause as ongoing, not just "present" — a condition that connects the past to right now, rather than something that only started existing today. Take "If I were more hardworking, I would have gotten that job last year": the reality is that I'm not hardworking — not now, and not back then either. That trait was untrue in the past and is still untrue in the present, which is exactly why something ongoing about me can explain something specific that happened last year. A one-off present state wouldn't be able to reach back and affect the past; an ongoing one can.

Common ongoing conditions in the 2&3 if-clause

Five broad categories cover almost everything that shows up here — hover over any pill for a sample sentence.

💡 "Could have" and "might have" work as alternatives to "would have" here too, exactly as in plain third conditional — and contractions apply throughout: "If I'd learned to swim," "she wouldn't be," "he'd have remembered."

🎯 The tricky part: matching a time-marker to the wrong clause type is the most common mistake.

❌ "If I had studied medicine, I would have been a doctor now." — wrong: "now" needs a present-form result ("would be"), not "would have been."
✅ "If I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now." — past condition (3), present result (2).

❌ "If she weren't so shy, she would speak up at yesterday's meeting." — wrong: "yesterday" needs a past-form result ("would have spoken").
✅ "If she weren't so shy, she would have spoken up at yesterday's meeting." — present trait (2), past result (3).

A quick check: look for a time word in the result clause. "Now"/"today" → present-form result. A specific past moment → "would have + past participle."

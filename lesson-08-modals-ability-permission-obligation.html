Modals of Ability, Permission, Obligation, and Prohibition

Can, could, must, have to, should, mustn't — four different jobs, easy to mix up.

1 Warm-up — a new kind of grammar

Every lesson so far has been about time — present, past, future. Today is different: modals aren't about when something happens, but about ability, permission, obligation, and prohibition.

1. Today's topic is about what someone…

2. "You must wear a helmet" and "you don't have to wear a helmet" are…

3. "She can speak three languages" is about…

💡 Same word, "can," covers ability AND permission depending on context. Today's whole lesson is about telling these jobs apart.

3 The concept

Four different jobs

Ability is about what someone is capable of. Permission is about what's allowed. Obligation is about what's required — or specifically not required. Prohibition is about what's actively forbidden, which is a very different thing from simply not required. Browse each below, switch between present and past, or test yourself with Quick check.

Present modals

✅ Ability: can / could / be able to / managed to + base verb — e.g. "She can speak three languages."
✅ Permission: can / could / may + base verb — e.g. "Visitors may take photos in the lobby."
✅ Obligation: must / have to / need to / should + base verb — e.g. "All staff must complete the training."
✅ Prohibition: mustn't / can't + base verb — e.g. "Visitors mustn't smoke inside the building."
🎯 The tricky part: "don't have to" (no obligation) and "mustn't" (prohibition) are not opposite ends of the same scale — they answer completely different questions.

✅ "You don't have to wear a suit." — it's simply not required. Wear one if you want to. No obligation.
✅ "You mustn't wear jeans to the ceremony." — jeans are actively forbidden. Prohibition.

"Don't have to" removes the requirement; "mustn't" adds a ban. Mixing these up is the single most common mistake at this level.

Past modals

Most modals don't inflect for the past the normal way — some borrow a completely different word instead, and two of them have no past form of their own at all.

✅ Ability (past): could (a general ability) / was-were able to, managed to (one specific achievement) — e.g. "She could read by age four." / "I was able to fix it just in time."
✅ Permission (past): could / was-were allowed to — e.g. "We were allowed to leave early that day."
✅ Obligation (past): had to — "must" has no past form of its own — e.g. "All staff had to complete the training before the launch."
✅ Prohibition (past): wasn't/weren't allowed to — "mustn't" has no past form of its own — e.g. "Visitors weren't allowed to bring bags inside."
🎯 The tricky part: "must" and "mustn't" simply don't have a past-tense version of themselves.

✅ Obligation: had to fills the gap, not "musted" — "All staff had to sign a new contract."
✅ Prohibition: wasn't/weren't allowed to fills the gap, not a past form of "mustn't" — "We weren't allowed to use our phones during the exam."

And remember: for a one-time past achievement (not a general ability), "could" alone doesn't work — you need "was/were able to" or "managed to." This tip may come in handy when you get to the gap-fill below.

📌 A later lesson covers modal perfect ("should have," "must have," "could have") — a different structure used for judging or guessing about the past, not simply describing it. That's coming soon.

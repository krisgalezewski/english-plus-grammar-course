3 The concept

Reporting what's widely believed

Reporting structures let you state a widely-held belief or claim without saying exactly who holds it — ideal for news, research, and formal writing where the source is vague, plural, or simply beside the point. Two patterns do the same job, one starting with "It," one starting with the actual subject. Switch to "Examples" below and scroll through — only the reporting verb ever changes.

🎯 The tricky part: both patterns report the SAME claim — pick whichever fits the sentence you're building.

✅ "It is thought that she wrote the letter herself." — starts with "it," full clause after "that."
✅ "She is thought to have written the letter herself." — starts with the real subject, "to have + past participle" for the past.

The most common mistake is mixing the two:
❌ "She is thought that she wrote the letter." — wrong: once the subject starts the sentence, drop "that" and switch to the infinitive.

Shifting the belief, and shifting the event

Two separate things can move into the past here, independently: when the belief was held ("is" → "was") and when the described event happened, relative to that belief (a simple form → a perfect one). Tap any sentence below to see exactly what's shifted.

Getting things done for you

Causative "have/get something done" describes an action you arranged for someone else to do — not something you did yourself. Same passive skeleton (past participle), new auxiliary.

— Click any part of the sentence to see another example.

"Get" — the informal alternative

Tap either sentence to compare.

🎯 The tricky part: plain passive and causative can look almost identical — the difference is entirely about arrangement.

✅ "My window was broken." — plain passive. It just happened; I didn't arrange it (probably an accident or vandalism).
✅ "I had my window fixed." — causative. I deliberately organised for someone to fix it.

If nobody arranged for it to happen, it's plain passive. If someone (usually the subject) organised it, it's causative.

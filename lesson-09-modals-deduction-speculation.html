3 The concept

How confident are you?

Deduction modals aren't facts — they're conclusions drawn from evidence or logic. The word you pick signals exactly how confident that conclusion is: must for near-certain, can't for confidently ruling something out, might/could/may for genuine uncertainty. Present

✅ Strong positive: must + base verb — e.g. "That must be her — she's the only one with a key."
✅ Strong negative: can't + base verb — e.g. "He can't be at work — I just saw him at the gym."
✅ Uncertain: might / could / may + base verb — e.g. "She might be running late."
🎯 The tricky part: deductive "must" doesn't have a normal negative. "Must not be" sounds like it should mean the opposite of "must be," but it doesn't work that way here.

✅ "That must be John." — confident positive conclusion.
✅ "That can't be John." — confident negative conclusion (John is ruled out).
❌ "That must not be John." — this reads as an order ("he mustn't be John," which makes no sense) rather than a deduction.

For confident certainty that something ISN'T true, always reach for can't, never "mustn't." This is the single most common trap at this level.

Past

The exact same logic applies to deductions about the past — you just add "have" and shift to the past participle. How confident you are still comes entirely from the modal you choose.

✅ Strong positive (past): must have + past participle — e.g. "She must have left already — her coat's gone."
✅ Strong negative (past): can't / couldn't have + past participle — e.g. "He can't have finished — he only started an hour ago."
✅ Uncertain (past): might / could / may have + past participle — e.g. "She might have missed the train."
🎯 The tricky part: just like in the present, "mustn't have" is not the negative of "must have" here.

✅ "She must have left already." — confident positive, about the past.
✅ "She can't have left already — I just saw her." — confident negative, about the past.
❌ "She mustn't have left already." — this reads as an order about the past, which makes no sense, rather than a deduction.

For confident certainty that something DIDN'T happen, always reach for can't have / couldn't have, never "mustn't have."

Will vs. Going To vs. Present Continuous

Three ways to talk about the future — and they're not interchangeable.

3 The concept

Three futures, three jobs

All three forms below point at the future — the difference is what's actually behind the sentence. Will is for the moment itself: a decision, a promise, or an opinion. Going to is for something you can already see coming — evidence now, or a plan made earlier. Present continuous is for something that's already locked into a schedule. ✅ Will: subject + will/'ll + base verb — e.g. "Don't worry, I'll help you with that."
✅ Going to: subject + am/is/are + going to + base verb — e.g. "Look at those clouds — it's going to rain."

🎯 The tricky part — will vs. going to: both can predict the future, but the source of your confidence is different.

✅ "I think it will rain later." — a general opinion, no particular evidence. Will.
✅ "Look at that sky — it's going to rain any minute." — you can see it coming, right now. Going to.

Going to also covers a plan you made earlier, even with no fixed time yet: "I've decided — I'm going to retrain next year." No calendar entry, just a firm intention.
🎯 The tricky part — going to vs. present continuous: both can describe a plan, but only one of them is actually booked.

✅ "I'm going to change my flight sometime this year." — an intention, no confirmed date yet. Going to.
✅ "I'm changing my flight on Tuesday — I already called the airline." — a specific day, already arranged with someone else. Present continuous.

If you could point to a line in a diary or a calendar, it's present continuous. If it's still just a decision in your head, it's going to.

Wait — didn't present continuous already mean "now"?

Good catch if you noticed that. Back in Lesson 1, am/is/are + verb-ing described something happening right at this moment — "She's working from home today." That exact same form is doing a completely different job here. This is normal in English: the same structure can serve several purposes, and what tells you which one is meant isn't the verb form itself — it's the time expression and context around it. No time marker (or a "now"-type one) usually means the present; a specific future day or time usually means a fixed future arrangement. Tap the sentences below to compare.

3 The concept

What is a relative clause?

A relative clause adds information about a noun mentioned in the sentence — a person, a place, a thing, or an idea. It usually starts with a relative pronoun (who, which, that, whose, whom) or a relative adverb (where, when, why) — hover over any of them for a sample sentence — and its job is to combine two related pieces of information into one smoother sentence, instead of two short separate ones.

For example, instead of two separate sentences — "I have a neighbour. She lends me her tools." — a relative clause combines them into one: "I have a neighbour who lends me her tools."

There are two very different types, and telling them apart matters for both meaning and punctuation: defining relative clauses identify exactly who or what you mean, and non-defining ones just add an extra, non-essential detail about someone or something already clear. We'll look at each in turn.

Defining — no commas, essential information

A defining relative clause gives essential information — it identifies exactly which person or thing you mean, and without it, the sentence would be unclear. Defining clauses are never separated by commas, and the noun they describe isn't always right at the start of the sentence — hover over the examples below to see both the noun and the clause highlighted, wherever they fall.

The relative pronoun can be the subject of its own clause ("the woman who called") or the object ("the book that I read"). When it's the object, it can often be left out entirely: "the book (that) I read was great." In everyday speech, "that" is very commonly used instead of "who" or "which" — both are correct.

💡 One thing worth watching for: when the relative pronoun is the subject of its clause, don't add another pronoun for the same person inside the clause. "She's the woman who lent me her umbrella" is correct — not "she's the woman who she lent me her umbrella," since "who" is already filling that role.

Non-defining — commas, extra information

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information — an interesting or useful detail, but not essential to understanding who or what is meant. The sentence still makes complete sense without it. Non-defining clauses are always set off with commas, and they never use "that" — only "who," "which," "whose," or "whom."

Where exactly do the commas go? That depends on where the clause sits in the sentence:

  • If the clause comes in the middle of the sentence — splitting the main clause into two parts — it needs two commas, one on each side, to clearly mark where it starts and stops.
  • If the clause comes at the end of the sentence, it only needs one comma, right before it, since the sentence simply finishes there — there's nothing left to "close off."

🎯 The tricky part: in defining clauses, you can only drop the relative pronoun if it's the object of the clause, not the subject.

✅ "The book (that) I read was great" — "that" is the object ("I read the book"), so it's optional.
❌ "The book arrived yesterday was a gift" — wrong: "that" was the subject here ("that arrived"), and subjects can never be dropped.
✅ "The book that arrived yesterday was a gift" — the subject pronoun has to stay.

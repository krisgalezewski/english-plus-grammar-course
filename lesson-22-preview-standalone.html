Putting the spotlight where you want it: cleft sentences

A cleft sentence rebuilds an ordinary sentence around "it" or a wh-word to push one particular part into the spotlight — the rest of the content stays exactly the same, it just moves. "What" is the most common choice, but "where," "why," "how," and "when" all work the same way, each pulling out whichever piece of information they naturally ask about. Switch between the forms below: blue always marks the answer being emphasised, amber always marks the describing clause — watch where each color travels as you switch.

"What," "who," and "which" can each emphasise either the subject or the object of the original sentence — hover over any pair below to see both. "Where," "why," "how," and "when" don't have this subject/object split since they emphasise a place, reason, method, or time instead, so each gets just one example. A caution on "who" and "which": both are grammatical here but noticeably rarer and more stilted-sounding than "what" — in real usage, it-cleft is usually preferred for emphasising a specific person or a specific choice from a set.

🎯 The tricky part: in an it-cleft, the verb after "it" agrees with "it" (always singular), not with the emphasised noun.

❌ "It were the advisors who ignored the signs." — wrong: "it" needs "was," regardless of what follows.
✅ "It was the advisors who ignored the signs."

And a wh-cleft's second verb has to match the real subject of the original sentence, not "what": "What caused the delay was poor planning" (singular "was," matching "poor planning," not "what").

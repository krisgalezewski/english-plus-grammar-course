Conditionals: Zero & First

Always-true facts, and one realistic possibility about the future.

3 The concept

Two kinds of "if"

Zero conditional states something that's always true — a fact, a rule, a habit. First conditional describes one specific, realistic possibility about the future. Same word "if," completely different job. In both, the two clauses can swap places — try the switch button below.

Zero conditional — general truth

💡 Notice how the widget handles this: when the same thing is named in both clauses ("ice"/"it," "plants"/"they"), the actual name always goes in whichever clause comes first, and the pronoun goes in whichever comes second — no matter which clause that happens to be. "Plants die if they don't get enough light" works; "They die if plants don't get enough light" doesn't, because that "they" has nothing to point back to yet.

First conditional — real future possibility

View
— Click either clause to see another example.
🎯 The tricky part: the if-clause never uses "will," in either conditional.

❌ "If it will rain tomorrow, we'll stay inside." — wrong: "will" doesn't belong in the if-clause.
✅ "If it rains tomorrow, we'll stay inside." — present simple in the if-clause, always.

And the second trap: zero and first conditional aren't about HOW LIKELY something is — they're about whether it's a general truth or one specific situation. "If a glass is dropped, it breaks" (zero — true of any glass, any time) is very different from "If you drop this glass, it'll break" (first — a prediction about one specific glass, right now).

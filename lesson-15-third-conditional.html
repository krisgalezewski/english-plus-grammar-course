3 The concept

A different past that never happened

Third conditional speculates about an alternate version of the past — the real event is over and can't change, but we imagine how it would have gone differently. The two clauses can still swap places — try the switch button below.

View
— Click either clause to see another example.

💡 In natural speech and informal writing, both clauses are usually contracted. "If I had studied" often becomes "If I'd studied," and "I would have passed" often becomes "I'd have passed" (or, less formally, "I would've passed"). Watch out for one thing: "I'd" is ambiguous on its own — it stands for "I had" in the if-clause and "I would" in the result clause. What follows always makes it clear: "I'd studied" (a past participle straight after "'d") is "had"; "I'd have studied" ("'d" + "have") is "would."

Alternative modals in the result clause

"Would have" is the default, but "could have" and "might have" fit naturally too. Hover over any example.

Where this connects

Third conditional is really just two things you already know, working together. Hover over any example.

🎯 The tricky part: mixing up second and third conditional is the single most common mistake here — both use "would," but the if-clause tense is completely different.

❌ "If I studied harder, I would have passed." — wrong: mixes second conditional's if-clause (present-hypothetical) with third conditional's result.
✅ "If I had studied harder, I would have passed." — third conditional needs past perfect throughout the if-clause, matching the past perfect result.

A simple test: is the event completely over and unchangeable? Third conditional. Is it still open, even if unlikely? Second conditional.

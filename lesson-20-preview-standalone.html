Gerunds vs. Infinitives

Which verbs take -ing, which take to + verb — and the handful that change meaning depending which you choose.

1 Warm-up — two ways to follow a verb

After many verbs, a second verb has to take one of two forms: the -ing form (a gerund) or "to + base verb" (an infinitive). Which one you need depends entirely on the first verb — there's no general rule, just patterns worth learning.

3 The concept

What are gerunds and infinitives?

A gerund is the -ing form of a verb used as a noun — it can be the subject of a sentence, the object of a verb, or follow a preposition, exactly the way an ordinary noun would. "Swimming is great exercise" uses "swimming" as the subject, the same job "football" or "exercise" would do there.

An infinitive is "to" plus the base form of a verb — "to swim," "to see," "to work." Like a gerund, it can act as a noun ("To swim was all I wanted"), but it also shows up expressing purpose ("I called to apologise") or following certain adjectives ("It's important to arrive on time").

Both are non-finite forms — verbs used without conjugating them for tense or person — which lets a verb idea function more like a noun, or add extra information to a sentence. Instead of two short ideas — "I enjoy something. I read before bed." — a gerund lets you combine them naturally: "I enjoy reading before bed."

The genuinely tricky part, and the focus of this lesson: after many verbs, only one of the two forms is grammatically correct, and there's no logical rule that predicts which — it comes down to the specific verb, and often just has to be learned.

Make, let, help — the bare infinitive

A small group of verbs takes an object followed by the base form of the verb, with no "to" at all — this is called the bare infinitive. Hover over any verb.

Going further

A few more patterns worth knowing, once the core distinction above feels comfortable.

Verb + object + infinitive

Some verbs can't jump straight from the verb to "to" — they need a person or thing (an object) sitting in between. "I want to help" (I'll do the helping) is a completely different sentence from "I want you to help" (someone else will). Hover over any verb.

Object + gerund

A few gerund-only verbs (can't stand, dislike, imagine, involve, mind, miss, put off, risk) work the same way in reverse: they can take a new subject — someone or something other than the sentence's main subject — right before the -ing form. If that new subject is a pronoun, it takes the object form (me, him, her, us, them), not the subject form. Hover over any verb.

Feel, hear, see, watch — in progress or complete?

A group of perception verbs (feel, hear, notice, see, overhear, watch) can also take an object followed by either -ing or a bare infinitive — and here the choice genuinely changes the meaning. The -ing form emphasises the action in progress, possibly repeated or unfinished; the bare infinitive emphasises the whole event, start to finish, as a single completed action. Hover over either sentence in a pair.

Gerunds show up elsewhere too

Two more places a gerund is the only option: right after a preposition, and as the subject of a sentence.

After a preposition

A preposition is always followed by -ing, never a to-infinitive. Hover over any phrase.

And a gerund can open a sentence as its subject: "Procrastinating rarely makes a task feel any easier." "Exercising regularly improves mood." A to-infinitive can technically do the same job ("To err is human"), but it reads far more formal, and modern English reaches for the gerund almost every time.

Making either form negative

"Not" simply goes right before the gerund or infinitive, whichever the verb calls for: "I regret not telling him the truth" (negative gerund) vs. "I decided not to go" (negative infinitive).

🎯 The tricky part: when a verb is followed directly by a preposition ("interested in," "good at," "afraid of"), the preposition rule wins — it's always gerund, never infinitive, no matter what the main verb usually prefers.

❌ "I'm interested to learn French." — wrong: "interested" is followed by "in," and "in" demands a gerund.
✅ "I'm interested in learning French."

This trips people up because "interested" feels similar in meaning to verbs like "want" or "hope," which do take a to-infinitive directly — but "interested in" isn't a verb pattern at all, it's adjective + preposition, and prepositions never take "to + verb."

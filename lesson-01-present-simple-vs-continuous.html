3 The concept

Two very different jobs

Both tenses talk about "now" in a loose sense — but they answer different questions. Present Simple answers "What's generally/always true?" Present Continuous answers "What's happening right now, or around now, temporarily?" Browse each below, or test yourself with Quick check.

Choosing the wrong one doesn't just sound "off" — it changes the meaning. "I work with data" (my job, always) is a completely different claim from "I'm working with data" (this specific project, temporarily).

✅ Present Simple: subject + base verb (+s) — e.g. "The team meets on Mondays."
✅ Present Continuous: subject + am/is/are + verb-ing — e.g. "The team is meeting right now."

Present Simple

Present Continuous

🎯 The tricky part: words like currently, these days, this year and at the moment can honestly go with either tense — they don't decide it by themselves. What matters is the question underneath: is this a stable fact/role (→ simple), or a temporary, in-progress situation (→ continuous)?

✅ "She currently manages a team of six." — this is just a fact about her job right now; it's presented as her ongoing role, not something changing moment to moment. Simple.
✅ "She's currently managing the London project." — a specific, temporary assignment that will end. Continuous.

Same word ("currently"), same person — different tense, because the situation being described is different. Pay attention to this exact contrast in the practice section below.

Stative verbs — verbs that resist "-ing"

A group of verbs describe states (thoughts, feelings, senses, possession) rather than actions — we almost never put them in the continuous form, even when talking about right now: ✅ "I understand" — ❌ "I am understanding." Common ones: know, believe, want, love, own, seem.

Tap any verb to hear it and see an example. Verbs marked * can occasionally be dynamic too, with a different meaning — see "Tricky cases" below.
